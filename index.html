The Big Question

How can you tell if something is living or nonliving?

A robot can move. A volcano releases gas. A sponge absorbs water. A chrysalis just sits there — yet it becomes a butterfly. By the end of this lesson, you'll have the scientific tools to answer this question for anything.

What you'll be able to do

Sort any object as living or nonliving — and justify your choice
Name and explain the 7 characteristics of life
Observe cells under a microscope and draw evidence-based conclusions
Write a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning statement from your own data

Build the Science — In YOUR Words

The 7 Characteristics of Life — discover each one

🔍Tap each card to reveal the characteristic. Then explain it to a partner in your own words before moving on.

These 7 things must ALL be true for something to be considered alive. Remember your microscope observations — which ones did you actually SEE?

🧱
Made of Cells
tap to reveal
All living things are made of one or more cells — the basic unit of life. This is what you saw under the microscope in the cheek and onion slides!
⚡
Uses Energy
tap to reveal
All living things use energy to carry out life processes. Plants get energy from sunlight; animals get it from food.
🌱
Grows & Develops
tap to reveal
Living things grow and change in an organized, predictable way — like a chrysalis developing into a butterfly.
🔁
Reproduces
tap to reveal
All living things can produce offspring — either sexually or asexually. Non-living things cannot reproduce.
🌡️
Responds to Stimuli
tap to reveal
Living things react to changes in their environment — a plant turning toward light, or your hand pulling away from heat.
⚖️
Maintains Homeostasis
tap to reveal
Living things keep their internal conditions stable — like your body keeping its temperature at 37°C even when it's cold outside.
🧬
Has DNA
tap to reveal
All living things contain DNA — the genetic blueprint that carries information and gets passed to offspring.

E4 · Elaborate
Apply It to the Real World
~10 minutes · Scenario-based application

Real-world scenarios — discuss with a partner, then write

💬Read each scenario. Discuss with a partner for 1 minute, then write your reasoned answer below it.
🤖
Scenario 1: A robot can move, sense its environment, and "eat" electricity. Your friend says "robots are alive!" Use the 7 characteristics to argue whether they are right or wrong.
Which characteristics does a robot have? Which does it LACK? Is every characteristic needed?
🌋
Scenario 2: A volcano releases gases, grows in size, and responds to pressure underground. A student classifies it as "living." Evaluate their reasoning using scientific evidence.
A volcano seems to do some "living" things — but does it meet ALL 7 characteristics?
🦠
Scenario 3: Scientists discovered a tiny object on Mars that appears to have a cell-like structure and contains DNA. Does this mean it's alive? What additional evidence would scientists need?
Think about all 7 characteristics — which ones would be hardest to test from a distance?
🧫
Scenario 4: A seed has been stored in a freezer for 100 years — no growth, no movement, no apparent energy use. Is it living or nonliving? Justify your answer.
Think about: can something be "alive" but dormant? What does the seed CONTAIN that matters?

Technology connection — discuss with your class

💬Share one idea with the class — choose any of the questions below.
🔭 Scientists use telescopes to find planets millions of km away. Similarly, we use microscopes to find cells. Why did it take hundreds of years for scientists to discover cells?
🌊 Ocean algae are microscopic — yet they produce 50% of Earth's oxygen. How does knowing that all living things are made of cells change how you think about tiny organisms?
🦠 Viruses have DNA but cannot reproduce on their own — they need a host cell. Are viruses living? Debate this with your partner using the 7 characteristics.
